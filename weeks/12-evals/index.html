Week 12 — Evals ​

An eval is a measuring instrument, and so is an LLM judge: before you trust a number from either, measure the instrument against human labels and say how uncertain it is.

Learning objectives ​

By the end of this week you can:

  1. Design an eval from failure modes: cases, metrics, a dev and a held-out split, error bars, and a paired comparison between versions.
  2. Explain why percent agreement flatters an LLM judge, and compute and interpret Cohen's κ next to an always-pass baseline.
  3. Calibrate a judge against your own blind labels, reporting n, agreement, κ and how well it catches failures, with human–human agreement as the ceiling.
  4. Gate CI on recorded responses, set thresholds from a run, and say what the gate catches and what it misses.
  5. Question a benchmark claim: harness, what "pass" means, contamination, reliability and error bars.

Lab ​

The lab continues in your eswa-eval-harness repository. The kit's judge grades the free-text booking title against a three-point rubric. You label the 29 shipped titles yourself before seeing any verdict, then calibrate: the shipped judge agrees with human labels about 79% of the time, which sounds good until a judge that always says "pass" scores about 76%. Its κ is about 0.3; the baseline's is 0. Then you copy the kit's CI workflow, so every push replays the recordings and fails when a metric drops below its threshold, with no key and no cost.

Reading ​

Hamel Husain, "Using LLM-as-a-Judge For Evaluation: A Complete Guide" (revised 2026), the reading list's core pick. A practitioner's method: one domain expert gives binary pass/fail grades with a written critique, the judge prompt is iterated until it agrees with that expert, plan on about 100 labels per failure mode, and report true-positive and true-negative rates, because an always-"pass" judge agrees 95% of the time when 5% of outputs fail.

Reading response prompt (150–250 words, 🔴 AI off, on Canvas): The kit ships 29 human labels from one labeler, for one field. Using Husain's guide, say what a κ computed on those 29 items can tell you and what it can't. Name one step of his process that the kit's setup lacks, and how you would add it in this week's assignment. Then say whether you would let the shipped judge's verdict decide anything automatically, and why. End with one question for the Seminar.

Key ideas ​

  • An eval is an experiment: a sample of cases, a metric, error bars and a decision rule. Capability evals ask what a system can do; regression evals check that it still does what it did. Grade outcomes, not step sequences (Anthropic).
  • Start from failure modes, found by reading outputs. Grading outputs changes your criteria, and some appear only after you look (criteria drift).
  • Agreement flatters a judge when most outputs pass. κ = (observed agreement − chance agreement) / (1 − chance agreement): 1 is perfect, 0 is chance. Among 13 judges in one study, only the largest approached humans, and high agreement hid large score gaps (Thakur et al.).
  • Calibrate on blind labels, and report n. Label before you see the judge's verdicts; report agreement, κ, the always-pass baseline, and how many failures the judge catches. On 29 items, κ is very uncertain. Two humans' agreement on the same rubric is roughly the ceiling for any judge.
  • A judge reads untrusted text too, and some checks (is the title plain text?) belong in code.
  • A regression gate on recordings is cheap and narrow. It catches changes to the code after the model call (parsing, validation, repair, scoring). It can't see a provider updating its model, or inputs unlike your cases; a prompt change forces new recordings, in their own reviewed PR.
  • A threshold is a target. For every gated metric, ask what change would raise it and whether that change makes the feature better.
  • Benchmarks overstate. Filtering leaked solutions and weak tests cut a resolve rate from 12.47% to 3.97% (SWE-Bench+); models named the buggy file from the issue alone 76% of the time on SWE-bench, 53% elsewhere (Liang et al., contamination); maintainers merged 24.2 points fewer than the grader accepted (METR).

Check your understanding ​

  1. 95% of titles truly pass. What agreement and what κ does an always-pass judge get, and why?
  2. Your judge has κ 0.30 on 29 labels from one person. Write the sentence you'd put in a report.
  3. Your CI gate is green, and the provider has just updated the model behind your model ID. What did the gate check, and what didn't it?
  4. Why must labels be made before you look at the judge's verdicts?

If a tool is down ​

  • No network or no key: labeling, calibration and the CI gate all replay recordings. Only the judge change in the assignment needs live calls; use the lab machine, where a small model makes a cheap judge (Tool Handout: Tools by week).
  • GitHub Actions is down or out of minutes: standard runners are free on public repositories (troubleshooting). Meanwhile run npm run eval:check locally and keep its output; push when Actions is back.
  • Codespaces down: the kit runs on any laptop with Node 22 (sandboxes).

Going further ​

  • Shankar et al., "Who Validates the Validators?" (UIST 2024), the source of criteria drift; Wang, Guo, Gao et al., "Can LLMs Replace Human Evaluators?" (ISSTA 2025), judges for code.
  • Miller, "Adding Error Bars to Evals": standard errors, paired comparisons and how many cases you need.
  • CS [5xxx]: Thakur et al. and Liang et al. are on the replicable list.
