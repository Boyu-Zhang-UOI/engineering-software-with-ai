Week 9 — Review at Scale ​

When agents write code faster than people can read it, review becomes both the bottleneck and the control: measure your reviewers, human and AI, against known defects, and turn the checks machines do well into gates.

Learning objectives ​

By the end of this week you can:

  1. Review an AI-authored PR systematically: treat its description as claims, check them by running the code, leave located comments marked blocking or not, and decide.
  2. Score reviewers, yourself and an AI reviewer, against a defect key with precision and recall and a confusion table, and explain why noise costs as much as misses.
  3. Interpret field evidence on AI review: merge rates when only bots review, how often comments lead to changes, and why studies disagree about cycle time.
  4. Set up required CI gates on a real fork (tests, lint, CodeQL or Semgrep), and keep workflows safe when other people's PRs run them.
  5. Write review comments that people act on.

Lab ​

The review lab uses roomly's review-prs/: five PRs as patches, each with its "author's" description (claims, not facts). The patches apply only to roomly as shipped, so you work in a fresh copy at your repository's first commit (a new clone or a git worktree). You review first, AI off; an AI reviewer follows; once your reviews are frozen, staff release the defect key and you score both. The assignment adds a CI gate on your team fork.

Reading ​

Chowdhury, Banik, Ferdous & Shamim, "From Industry Claims to Empirical Reality" (MSR 2026; free on arXiv). PRs reviewed only by code-review agents were merged 45.2% of the time, against 68.4% for human-reviewed PRs, and were abandoned more often; 12 of 13 agents had average signal ratios below 60%. It tests the industry claim that agents can handle most PRs alone.

Reading response prompt (150–250 words, 🔴 AI off): State the main finding with two numbers. PRs that only a bot reviewed may differ from human-reviewed ones before any review happens: name one such difference and which way it would push the merge rates. Explain "signal ratio" in your own words, and why it matters as much as how many defects a reviewer finds. End with one question for the Seminar.

More: reading list, week 9.

Key ideas ​

  • Agent PRs get merged, then fixed. Of 567 Claude Code PRs, 83.8% were merged and 45.1% of those needed further changes (Watanabe et al.); maintainers merged test-passing agent PRs 24.2 points less often than a grader accepted them (METR). Green CI is where review starts.
  • Review burden is the cost that scales: making agent PRs mergeable took 42 minutes on average in METR's 2025 study. The REVIEW template asks for a PR small enough for one sitting, or a split.
  • AI reviewers' results disagree. At Beko, 73.8% of AI comments were resolved but PR closure time rose from 5 h 52 min to 8 h 20 min (Cihan et al.); at Atlassian, 38.7% of AI comments led to changes (humans: 44.45%) and cycle time fell 30.8% (Tantithamthavorn et al.).
  • Precision is the design problem. False alarms spend everyone's attention; Copilot's reviewer deliberately says nothing on 29% of reviews (GitHub).
  • Comments that lead to changes are concise, include a code snippet, or were asked for (Sun et al., 22,000+ comments): location, observation, consequence, fix, blocking or not.
  • Static analysis matches known dangerous shapes, and rarely finds logic errors. LLMs can feed it: LLM-inferred taint specifications let CodeQL find 55 of 120 Java CVEs, against 27 alone (IRIS).
  • A gate is a check that blocks merging. On old code, gate what a PR changes (a ratchet), or the gate is red forever and everyone learns to ignore it.
  • Other people's PRs run your workflows, with your fork's secrets: in the PR round, team forks hold none.

Check your understanding ​

  1. A PR has 4 known defects. A reviewer leaves 10 comments: 4 match defects (two the same one), 4 point at problems that aren't there, 2 are praise. Using the lab's counting rule, what are its precision and recall?
  2. How would you check a PR description's "no behavior change"?
  3. Name two differences between Beko and Atlassian that could explain their opposite cycle-time results.
  4. Your fork's linter reports 2,000 warnings on main. How do you add a lint gate that helps rather than blocks?
  5. Why is pull_request_target plus a checkout of the PR's code dangerous?

If a tool is down ​

  • The AI reviewer: CodeRabbit on a public repository first, then PR-Agent with your Gemini key (on your own roomly repository only), then Copilot code review at Lite effort (Tool Handout: Tools by week). If none works, report the blocker and score your own review only.
  • No CodeQL default setup (private repository, no admin rights): run Semgrep Community Edition, and ask staff for the setting. Failing workflows: troubleshooting.

Going further ​

  • Peralta et al., "Why Are Agentic Pull Requests Merged or Rejected?": only 35.7% of rejections were clear agent failures, so "rejected" is a noisy label for "bad".
  • Oliveira et al., arXiv 2604.23251: in a 100+-student capstone, failed AI reviews fell from 227 to 0 after tool and instruction fixes. Pilot your bot.
  • Case A8: agents publishing screenshots for code reviews.
