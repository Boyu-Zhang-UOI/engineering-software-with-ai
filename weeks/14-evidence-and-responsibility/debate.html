The Week 14 Debate ​

Five motions, five eight-minute rounds, and a rule that every claim names its source. Persuasion and evidence are scored separately, so we can see when they disagree.

ItemDetails
WhenThe week 14 Seminar, after the due-date quiz and an 8-minute talk on reading evidence
FormatFive rounds of 8 minutes, one motion each, with a vote before and after every round
RolesTwo speakers for, two against, one evidence judge per round; everyone else is the floor (roles)
SidesAssigned on Canvas after the week 13 Seminar. You may be arguing against your own view: that's the exercise
PreparationSpeakers post three evidence cards in the Canvas debate thread by 11:59 pm the night before; judges post the three numbers they'll check
AI use🔴 AI off for cards, speeches, rulings and questions. Read the sources yourself
Graded asSeminar participation, as usual (rubric). The scorecards are feedback, not grades

The motions ​

RoundMotionEvidence area
1This house believes AI coding agents make professional developers faster.Productivity
2This house believes the quality cost of AI-assisted code outweighs its output gains.Quality
3This house would switch coding agents off for a developer's first year, in a degree or in a job.Skills and critical thinking
4This house believes open-source projects should decline AI-generated contributions, as QEMU does, rather than accept disclosed ones, as LLVM does.Maintainers' policies and attribution
5This house believes that, for AI-assisted work on public open-source code, disclosure and human review are enough to handle licensing and privacy.Licensing, provenance and privacy

Roles ​

RoleHow many per roundWhat you do
Speaker for2Speaker 1 opens (90 seconds); speaker 2 closes (45 seconds), answering what the floor and the other side raised
Speaker against2The same, for the other side
Evidence judge1Reads the round's source pack in advance, scores every claim on the scorecard, and gives a 60-second evidence ruling: the best-supported claim, the weakest one, and one claim the evidence supports that nobody made
FloorEveryone elseVotes; may raise one point of evidence per round; asks questions in the floor block
ModeratorThe instructorKeeps time, enforces the rules, runs the votes

With about 25 students, everyone has one role in one round. With fewer than 15, use one speaker per side (opening and closing) and merge rounds 1 and 2 into "This house believes AI coding agents make software teams more productive". With more than 25, add a second judge per round. CS [5xxx] students are judges by default. If a graduate paper presentation is scheduled this week, it replaces the round on its topic: the presenter is the round's expert witness and the discussant opens its floor (paper presentations).

Everyone votes on all five motions before round 1 (a Canvas poll, or hands). The swing for each motion is the change between the two votes.

Rules ​

  1. Name the source in the sentence. "METR's 2025 trial found…", not "studies show…".
  2. Label opinion as opinion. "In my opinion…" or "I infer…" is allowed and never penalized; opinion presented as fact is.
  3. Say what kind of source it is. A preprint is a preprint; a vendor's post is the vendor's; a survey measures what people report.
  4. Numbers come with their uncertainty when the source gives one (a confidence interval or a standard error).
  5. Points of evidence. Anyone on the floor may say "Point of evidence" once per round. The speaker has 15 seconds to name the source; if they can't, the judge strikes the claim.
  6. Claims about our own code, agents or tools are tagged observed or inferred.
  7. Argue evidence, not people. No comments on classmates' work, and maintainers are discussed only through what their projects published.
  8. Time is strict. The moderator stops you mid-sentence; that's what the closing is for.

Evidence cards ​

Each speaker posts three cards in the Canvas debate thread by 11:59 pm the night before the Seminar, so the judge can check them. At least one must come from a peer-reviewed paper or a primary document (a policy, a court record), and at least one must be against interest: a real limitation of your own side's best evidence. Conceding it out loud earns a point.

text
Card [round]-[for/against]-[1-3] · [GitHub username]
Claim (the sentence you will say):
Source: [authors or project, title, venue or kind (peer-reviewed, preprint, vendor, policy, news, court), year, link]
Design: [randomized trial · field experiment · difference-in-differences · observational · survey · interviews · benchmark · policy text · court record]
Sample: [who or what, and how many]
Number: [the figure, with its uncertainty if the source gives one]
Limit: [one thing this source can't show]
Against interest: [yes / no]

Scoring ​

The judge scores every claim a speaker makes aloud. The side with more evidence points wins the evidence; the swing is reported separately.

PointsWhen
+2Source named, number accurate (with its uncertainty where given), design described correctly
+1Source named, but the number is missing or rounded in a misleading way
+1A real limitation of your own evidence, conceded without being asked
0Opinion, labeled as opinion
−1A claim presented as fact without a source, or struck after a point of evidence
−2Misstated evidence: a wrong number, a correlation stated as a cause, a preprint called peer-reviewed, a vendor claim called independent

Source packs ​

Starting points, with the numbers as recorded in the course's evidence notes. They are not a script: read the source before you cite it, and you may bring others from the reading list.

Round 1 — Productivity ​

  • METR's 2025 randomized trial (paper; summary): 16 experienced developers, 246 tasks in large, mature repositories they knew well. Tasks with AI allowed took 19% longer (interval roughly +2% to +39%); developers had forecast a 24% speedup and afterwards believed in about 20%.
  • METR's February 2026 update (post): 57 developers, 800+ tasks. Task time −18% for returning developers (interval −38% to +9%) and −4% for new ones (−15% to +9%). Some developers declined or dropped out rather than work without AI, and 30–50% withheld tasks they thought needed AI, so METR calls the estimates unreliable and the true speedup probably underestimated.
  • Cui et al., Management Science 2026 (paper): randomized rollouts at three firms, 4,867 developers: +26.08% completed tasks (SE 10.3%); juniors +27–39%, seniors +8–13%; about 60% adoption after a year; quality not observed.
  • Paradis et al. (Google, preprint; arXiv): 96 engineers, about 21% less time, wide interval.
  • Murphy-Hill et al. (Microsoft, preprint; arXiv): adopters of command-line agents merged about 24% more PRs than their counterfactual, for four months.
  • DORA 2025 (report): about 5,000 respondents; 90% use AI at work and over 80% believe it raised their productivity; adoption correlates with higher throughput and lower stability. A survey, not a trial.
  • METR's 2026 survey (post): 349 workers reported a median of about 3× speed; METR notes earlier work found people overestimate time savings by about 40 percentage points.

Round 2 — Quality ​

  • He et al., MSR 2026 (arXiv): 807 Cursor-adopting repositories against 1,380 matched controls. Lines added rose 281% in the first month and the gain was gone after about two months; static-analysis warnings (+29.7%) and complexity (+40.7% in Table 2, 25.1% in the text) stayed up.
  • Denisov-Blanch et al. (preprint; arXiv): in 441 repositories, agents raised commits 28–38%; complexity rose 53% in agent-first repositories with no committed AI configuration file vs 27% with one. Observational.
  • Borg et al., ICSME 2025 registered report (arXiv): AI cut completion time 30.7%, but other developers later evolving that code showed no significant difference in time or quality.
  • Watanabe et al. (preprint; arXiv): of 567 agent PRs, 83.8% merged, and 45.1% of those needed further changes.
  • METR, maintainers vs graders (note): four maintainers reviewed 296 test-passing agent PRs; their merge rate ran 24.2 points below the automated grader.

Round 3 — Skills and critical thinking ​

  • Shen & Tamkin (Anthropic randomized trial, preprint; arXiv; summary): 52 mostly junior engineers learning a new library. Quiz scores 50% with AI vs 67% without (Cohen's d = 0.738), the biggest gap on debugging; the AI group was not significantly faster. Participants who asked for explanations scored higher.
  • Balepur et al. (preprint; arXiv): 54 students finished faster with an editing agent but understood less and extended their code worse without AI, and still preferred the agent. No effect sizes in the abstract.
  • Lee et al., CHI 2025 (PDF): 319 knowledge workers, 936 examples: higher confidence in the AI predicted less critical thinking; higher self-confidence predicted more. Self-reported.
  • Cui et al. (round 1): juniors gained the most speed.
  • Brynjolfsson, Chandar & Chen (follow-up): employment of developers aged 22–25 down nearly 20% from the late-2022 peak; under stricter controls the decline is significant only after 2024, and partly driven by non-AI factors.
  • Yu & Moon, AIES 2026 (arXiv): 14 interviews describing "Absorption", entry-level work folded into senior-plus-AI workflows.

Round 4 — Maintainers' policies and attribution ​

  • QEMU (policy; case E1): declines contributions believed to include AI-generated content, citing copyright and certificate-of-origin uncertainty; a May 2026 proposal to allow small changes with an AI-used-for: trailer has no recorded decision.
  • Ghostty (policy; case E2): all AI use disclosed; contributors explain changes without AI; a public denouncement list.
  • LLVM (policy; case E3): read and review all generated code, an Assisted-by: trailer, self-written PR descriptions, no agents acting without human approval.
  • The Linux kernel (policy; Hackaday; case E4): "AI agents MUST NOT add Signed-off-by tags"; in October 2026 maintainers dropped the AI tag requirement, while networking maintainers run several models on each patch for review.
  • Hora & Robbes (preprint; arXiv): of 118 policies in 1,000 popular repositories, 78% allow AI-assisted contributions, 22% discourage AI use, 51% require disclosure and 74% a human in the loop.
  • The cost to maintainers (case E7): Hacktoberfest 2026 dropped its PR challenge after "a massive flood of activity and burnout"; curl ended its bug bounty.
  • Horikawa et al. (preprint; arXiv): across 7.8M commits, detectors of agent-written commits reach precision 0.332 at realistic prevalence: detection "cannot replace recorded attribution".

Round 5 — Licensing, provenance and privacy ​

  • LiCoEval, ICSE 2025 (arXiv): across 14 models, even the best produced 0.88–2.01% of outputs "strikingly similar" to existing open-source code, and most gave inaccurate license information, especially for copyleft.
  • Thaler v. Perlmutter, cert. denied 2026 (SCOTUSblog): the human-authorship requirement for copyright stands; the denial is not a ruling on AI-assisted works.
  • Bartz v. Anthropic (JURIST): training on lawfully bought books was held fair use, keeping 7M+ pirated books was not; settled for $1.5B, about $3,000 per work.
  • NetBSD and Gentoo (case E5): LLM output "presumed to be tainted code"; Gentoo bans it.
  • The data rule (Tool Handout: The data rule): several free tiers the course uses may train on prompts, some with no opt-out.
  • PixelLeak (case A8): agents asked for review screenshots created public repositories and exposed 13,000+ internal screenshots from 300+ organizations. No attacker.
  • GitHub MCP and CamoLeak (A2; A4): a public issue led an agent to copy private-repository data into a public PR; hidden PR comments leaked private code through image URLs (CVSS 9.6).

After the debate ​

The instructor closes with what the evidence supports, round by round, and where the evidence points and the swing disagreed. If you'd rather write than speak, a substantive comment in the Canvas debate thread within 24 hours counts as participation: for example, the claim that changed your mind and the source behind it.

Accommodations. Speaking is never required. Anyone can take the judge role with a written ruling the moderator reads, or contribute cards that a teammate speaks to. CDAR accommodations apply as usual; tell the instructor when roles are posted.
