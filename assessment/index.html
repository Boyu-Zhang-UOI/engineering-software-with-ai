Assessment ​

How your work is graded, and why. The question is never "did the agent produce something that runs?" It is "did you specify it, verify it, secure it, and can you explain it?"

This page covers grading for Engineering Software with AI (CS [4xxx], joint-listed with CS [5xxx], University of Idaho): 3 credits over a 16-week semester. A credit means about 45 hours of work (catalog Regulation D-1), so plan on about 135 hours (CS [5xxx]: about 160), or about 8.5 hours a week (CS [5xxx]: about 10): a 75-minute Seminar and a 75-minute Lab, plus about 6 hours outside class (CS [5xxx]: about 7.5). Weeks 1–15 are teaching weeks; week 16 is finals week, when the final defense runs in the course's scheduled final exam slot (Regulation H-1-b).

The scoring guides are in Rubrics, the oral exam protocol in Oral Exams, and the due-date quiz rules in Due-Date Quizzes. Team project deliverables are defined once, in Team Project Milestones, and the graduate work in graduate/. Everything is submitted on Canvas, the course's learning management system.

Grading philosophy ​

Evidence over output. Agents make output cheap. They don't make correctness, security or understanding cheap. Maintainers of three major Python projects merged test-passing agent PRs at a rate 24.2 points below what an automated grader accepted (METR), and students who built with an agent understood less of their code and extended it worse without AI (Balepur et al.). So the course grades the things that show engineering judgment:

  • Evidence of verification: tests whose adequacy is measured (properties, mutation scores), CI runs, security findings with regression tests, evals with calibrated judges.
  • Evidence of process: commit history, AI_LOG.md (what you specified, what you delegated, how you verified), PR descriptions and reviews you wrote yourself.
  • Explanation with the AI off: due-date quizzes, two oral exams and the individual questions at the final defense.
  • Honest limitations. UT Austin's CS 378 states the principle this course follows: "Feature count, repository size, token spend, prompt volume, and visual polish do not substitute for demonstrated behavior, reproducible checks, controlled engineering decisions, or honest limitations" (CS 378). Naming a real limitation never costs points.

No arms race. AI is required for code, so assignments aren't designed to be AI-proof, and the course doesn't use AI detectors. Understanding is checked in person instead, in short quizzes about your own submissions and in conversation.

Process, not merges. Nothing in your grade depends on an outside maintainer's decision. Michigan's EECS 481 found 44% of student open-source PRs accepted, and its authors advise to "avoid mandatory grading of whether pull requests are accepted" (Fang et al.). Team work is graded from snapshots of your team fork; upstream merges earn only a bonus.

How grades are calculated ​

Each component is scored in points, then scaled to its weight: points earned ÷ points possible × weight.

Reading responses and participation combine as component weight × (0.6 × responses ÷ 60 + 0.4 × participation ÷ 24).

Example (CS [4xxx]): 226 of 260 lab points is 226 ÷ 260 × 35 = 30.4% of the course. The same points for a CS [5xxx] student are 226 ÷ 260 × 30 = 26.1%.

Letter grades ​

The University of Idaho uses letter grades without plus or minus (Regulation E).

Your course percentage is rounded to the nearest whole number before the letter is assigned (89.5 becomes 90). Optional upstream merges can add a bonus on top (contribution rules).

To earn a C or higher, you must also have taken both oral exams and the final defense (the team demo and your individual questions). A retake or a rescheduled exam counts as taken. This condition applies to everyone, so it also works with the pass/fail option below. If it's at risk because of an extenuating circumstance, talk to me early: an Incomplete is possible (catalog Regulation F).

How to submit ​

  • Assignments: commit to your lab repository and submit, on Canvas, the repository URL and the commit (or tag) you want graded. We grade that commit as it was at the deadline. The week's lab.md lists the deliverables.
  • Lab checkoffs: in the Lab, to a staff member, who records them on Canvas.
  • Team milestones: one member submits the team fork's URL on Canvas by the deadline. At the deadline staff tag your fork's main (m0, m1, m2; final at the start of the final exam slot), record the open branches and PRs, and grade that snapshot. PRs don't need to be merged to count, as long as the milestone says so.
  • The PR round: your PR's URL and your review links, on Canvas.
  • Reading responses, reflections, peer evaluations, the final report and all graduate writing: on Canvas only, never in a repository (Privacy of your work).
  • Quizzes: on Canvas, in the Seminar, with AI tools closed (or on paper).

Due dates. Unless Canvas says otherwise, everything is due before next week's Seminar (default 11:59 pm the night before). Reading responses are due before the Seminar of their own week (week 1: before the week 1 Lab). The final report, peer evaluation 3 and the CS [5xxx] final paper are due by 11:59 pm on the last day of finals week, and always before the University's grade deadline.

Privacy of your work ​

Your code is public on purpose: the team forks, the PR round and any upstream work only make sense in the open. Your grades and your personal writing are not. University policy treats grades and graded work as private (FSH 4700 F-6, and FSH 2600, which applies FERPA, the US federal student-records law), so the course keeps the two apart.

In public repositories (under your GitHub username)On Canvas only
Code, tests and CI configuration; SPEC.md, DESIGN.md, TASKS.md, AGENTS.md, THREAT_MODEL.md, EVAL_PLAN.md, TEAM_CHARTER.md, AI_LOG.md; issues, PR descriptions and code review commentsReading responses; reflections; peer evaluations; the final report; graduate critiques, discussant notes, study protocols and papers; quiz and oral-exam results; the signed agent safety contract; all grades and feedback
  • Never commit grades, feedback, reflections, peer evaluations, classmates' real names (refer to people by GitHub username), your student ID, or real personal data about anyone.
  • You don't need your real name anywhere public. A GitHub username that doesn't reveal it is fine.
  • Code review on GitHub is ordinary code review. Keep it about the code. Grades and feedback come back on Canvas only; staff never post grades or feedback as issues, PR comments or commits.
  • Lab repositories may be public or private. If private, add the course staff as collaborators. Free CI and code scanning are most generous on public repositories (Tool Handout).
  • Recordings of oral exams or defense questions happen only with your consent, are kept where only staff can see them, and are deleted once grades are final. You may record a Seminar or Lab only with the instructor's consent or as an approved CDAR accommodation, and in either case must tell the instructor first (FSH 4700 F-8).
  • Class results (for example quiz statistics) are shared only as summaries in which no one can be identified.
  • After the course your lab repositories are yours. Team forks stay in the course organization until the end of the following term, then are archived; tell the instructor if you'd like a copy.

Late work and grace tokens ​

  • Two grace tokens per student. Each extends one individual deadline (an assignment, a reading response or a graduate deliverable other than a presentation or talk) by 48 hours, no reason needed. Write "grace token" in your Canvas submission comment; you don't need to ask first.
  • One team token per team. It extends M0, M1 or M2 by 48 hours for the whole team. Individual tokens can't be used on team milestones, and no token applies to the Final.
  • Tokens can't be used for live or fixed-clock events: due-date quizzes, oral exams, the PR round, Lab checkoffs, Seminar participation, paper presentations, study talks and the final defense. Those have make-up rules instead (below).
  • Tokens don't extend the week 9 review freeze. In week 9 you freeze your own reviews and the AI reviewer's output by submitting their commit hash on Canvas (default: 11:59 pm two days after the Lab), and staff release the defect key to you once your freeze is in. A token can extend the rest of that week's assignment, but not the freeze; changes to the frozen reviews after it are ignored (week 9).
  • No token moves work past the University's final-grade deadline.
  • After your tokens run out: late work loses 10% of that item's points per day, for up to 3 days. After that it scores zero unless you've agreed an extension.
  • Tool trouble is not by itself a reason to be late. UT Austin's course says it bluntly: a note that you "ran out of credit" doesn't excuse a missing homework. Switching to a fallback is part of the course (troubleshooting). If every fallback fails, keep screenshots of the errors and contact the instructor before the deadline; you won't be penalized.

Absences and make-up work ​

Attendance isn't scored on its own. It affects your grade through Seminar participation (2 points per Seminar, lowest three dropped) and the Lab checkoff (5 of each week's 20 lab points, lowest week dropped).

  • Absences the University recognizes (Regulation M-1): official university activities, your own illness, family illness or care, or other compelling circumstances. Tell the instructor as soon as you can (before the absence, for official activities). Then:
    • a missed Lab checkoff can be shown in office hours within one week for full credit;
    • a missed Seminar is excused (removed from the total);
    • a missed quiz is excused or made up within one week, whichever you prefer;
    • a missed oral exam is rescheduled without penalty and doesn't use up the retake;
    • a missed presentation, PR-round deadline or final defense is rescheduled or replaced by an equivalent task;
    • deadlines move without using a token.
  • Religious observance (Regulation M-3): quizzes, oral exams, presentations, PR-round deadlines and the final defense are rescheduled. Tell the instructor as early as you can.
  • Other absences: a Lab checkoff shown in office hours within 48 hours earns up to 3 of its 5 points. A missed quiz or Seminar scores zero; the drops are there to absorb a few of these.
  • The grade deadline is firm. Make-ups, retakes and resubmissions must fall before the University's final-grade deadline. If extenuating circumstances make that impossible, an Incomplete may be agreed (Regulation F).

Team grades and peer evaluation ​

The team project has team rows, scored once for the whole team, and individual rows, scored for each member (the rubrics mark which is which). Peer evaluation and contribution evidence can adjust only your share of the team rows.

  • The default is no adjustment. Every member receives the team rows' score.
  • Peer evaluations are submitted on Canvas, 🔴 AI off, with M1, M2 and the Final. You rate each teammate and yourself from 1 to 5 on five criteria (does their share, quality of their work, reviews others' work, communicates and shows up, helps the team decide), with one sentence of evidence for each rating (rubric). Ratings are seen only by staff. A missing peer evaluation costs you 2 points of that milestone.
  • Contribution evidence is what the repository shows: commits and PRs authored, reviews given, issues opened and discussed, AI_LOG.md entries, task ownership in TASKS.md, and, for the Final, the contribution table in the final report (by GitHub username).
  • When an adjustment happens. Only when the peer evaluations and the contribution evidence both show a substantial difference, for example a median peer rating below 3 for "does their share" together with no authored PR or review for the milestone. The M1 evaluation works as an early warning: if it shows a problem, the instructor meets the team, and the team's charter process applies first.
  • How. Before any adjustment, the member sees a summary of the concerns (not who said what) and can respond in writing within 7 days. If the instructor then adjusts, an individual factor between 0 and 1.0 multiplies the team rows of that milestone. Factors never exceed 1.0: adjustments protect teams from free-riding; they are not a bonus. Your individual rows are unaffected.
  • Evidence wins. Ratings that contradict the repository (a team that rates everyone 5 while one member has no commits, or one member rated low despite steady authored and reviewed PRs) are weighed against the record, not taken at face value.
  • If a team breaks down, tell the instructor early. Teams can be reorganized up to week 9; after that, members keep working on their own parts and the milestones are scored per member.

Feedback and regrades ​

  • Grades and written feedback come back on Canvas, aiming for within a week and before the next related deadline. Milestone feedback comes before the next milestone's work is due.
  • Regrade requests: within 7 days of getting a grade, in writing on Canvas, naming the rubric row and your reason. For team rows, the team sends one request. The whole item may be regraded, so the score can go up or down.
  • Quizzes: the answers are discussed in the Seminar or posted on Canvas the same day; regrade requests follow the same rule.

Pass/fail and incompletes ​

This course is letter-graded (A–F) for everyone.

The pass/fail option (Regulation B-10). Who may use it depends on your level and major: CS majors can't take CS [4xxx] pass/fail, and graduate students need the approval of their major professor and the dean of the College of Graduate Studies (details in the syllabus). Instructors aren't told who chose it: the instructor reports a letter grade, and the Registrar records a C or higher as P. For undergraduates, a D stays on the record as a D; for graduate students, a D is recorded as an F. The conditions for a C or higher are above.

Incompletes (Regulation F). An Incomplete is possible only if you have been attending and doing passing work until near the end (the regulation's cutoff is three weeks before the close of the semester) and an extenuating circumstance, such as serious illness or a death in the family, makes it impossible to finish on time. We agree it together; the instructor sets out in writing what's left to do and by when, and records a grade that applies if the work isn't completed. Running out of time because of procrastination doesn't qualify. For team work, the plan names what you complete on your own.

AI use policy ​

The full policy, including the complete list of 🔴 AI off and 🟡 AI limited work, is in section 9 of the syllabus. That list is the one that counts.

In short: 🟢 AI on for code, tests, exploration, CI and AI reviewers; 🟡 AI limited for SPEC.md, DESIGN.md, TASKS.md, AGENTS.md and THREAT_MODEL.md (you make the decisions and write them; an AI may critique, or explore and propose options), the final report (the limitations, reflection and contribution sections are yours) and the graduate study (analysis code may be AI-assisted and disclosed; the research question, the design and the prose are yours); 🔴 AI off for quizzes, oral exams, the final defense's individual questions, reading responses, reflections, peer evaluations, PR descriptions and review comments you sign, graduate critiques and discussant questions. If a brief and the syllabus ever seem to disagree, treat the stricter label as the rule until you have an answer.

  • Disclose: AI_LOG.md in every repository, an Assisted-by: [tool and model] trailer on AI-assisted commits, and the AI section of every PR description.
  • Own it: if you can't explain it, you didn't build it. You may be asked to explain any line you submit.
  • Stricter policies win: on someone else's project, its AI policy overrides the course's when it is stricter.

Academic integrity ​

Disclosure is the rule. Honest disclosure is never penalized, even when an agent did most of the typing.

What counts as academic dishonesty here. The U of I Student Code of Conduct and Resolution Process (FSH 2300, section E-1) covers it. In this course that includes:

  • using AI on 🔴 AI-off work, or beyond what a 🟡 AI-limited label allows ("reliance on prohibited technological assistance/artificial intelligence tools", FSH 2300 E-1.c);
  • fabricating evidence: test results, mutation scores, CI runs, security findings, eval numbers, study data or AI_LOG.md entries that didn't happen. Agents have reported tests as passing that never ran; you are responsible for every number you submit;
  • submitting someone else's code, prompts or writing as your own, including a generated PR description, review comment or reading response presented as yours;
  • misrepresenting contributions: in a peer evaluation, a contribution table or a commit's authorship;
  • submitting work made for another course without the prior approval of both instructors;
  • having someone else take your quiz, oral exam or defense questions.

There are two separate processes, and they never mix.

1. "Explain it or redo it": a learning conversation, not a sanction. Sometimes you can't yet explain part of your own work, or AI_LOG.md doesn't seem to match what's in the repository. Then the instructor asks for a short conversation, about 10 minutes, like an oral exam. Nobody is alleging misconduct. Usually you can explain it and that's the end of it. If you can't yet, you redo that part within 7 days: study or rebuild it, update AI_LOG.md, and explain it in a short follow-up. The part is then graded on the normal rubric, with no cap and no penalty for needing the follow-up. If, during a conversation, the instructor comes to suspect misconduct, they will stop, tell you so, and use the University process below instead.

2. Suspected academic dishonesty: the University's process. If the instructor suspects academic dishonesty, they will tell you and notify the Dean of Students, as FSH 2300 F-9.b.1 requires. No grade consequence is applied until that process has finished and its decision has been communicated to you (F-9.b.10). If grades are due before then, an Incomplete is entered until it's resolved (F-9.b.11). The Dean of Students explains your options and rights, including an informal resolution if everyone agrees to one.

We don't use AI detectors. Their verdicts can't be checked or explained, and even detectors built for agent-written commits reach only about one-third precision at realistic rates (Horikawa et al.). A conversation about your work tells us what we need to know.

Accommodations ​

If you believe you need disability-related academic adjustments, contact the Center for Disability Access and Resources (CDAR). We follow CDAR accommodation letters. Also tell the instructor in week 1 if a health condition, time zone, device or connectivity issue could affect your work. Many adjustments are open to everyone, with or without a letter:

  • Oral exams: extra time, a written-first version, typed instead of spoken answers, breaks, a quiet room, camera off, your own assistive technology (details).
  • Due-date quizzes: extra time (start before the Seminar or finish in a nearby room, so you don't miss the concept talk), screen-reader-friendly or large-print forms (details).
  • Final defense: your individual questions in a private slot instead of in front of the class (details).
  • Presentations (CS [5xxx]): a pre-recorded talk with live discussion, if presenting live is a barrier.
  • Deadlines: extensions as set out in your accommodation letter, on top of your grace tokens.
  • Tools: Codespaces runs in a browser on modest laptops. If your connection is unreliable, talk to the instructor about campus machines.
