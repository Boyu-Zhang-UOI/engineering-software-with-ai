Landscape Report: Teaching Agentic Software Engineering to Seniors and Graduate Students ​

November 2026. A synthesis of the three research notes behind Engineering Software with AI: comparable courses, the $0 tool landscape (checked 2026-11-02) and the evidence base. Every claim links to its source; where a source is a preprint, a vendor or a search summary, the text says so. This is a dated record: current tool facts live in Tool Handout, and the decisions this report led to are in Why the Course Looks Like This.

On this page: Summary · 1. Comparable courses · 2. Team projects on real codebases · 3. Graduate differentiation · 4. The evidence base · 5. The $0 tool landscape · 6. Design implications · 7. Gaps

Summary ​

  1. Upper-division courses on agentic software engineering now exist, and they agree on more than they differ. UT Austin's CS 378 frames the work as deciding "what you specify, what you delegate, and what you verify", teaches prompt injection and sandboxing in week 3, opens each homework due date with a 7-minute AI-free quiz, and ends with a live defense graded on evidence rather than feature count (CS 378). Michigan's EECS 498 runs at $0 on local models and moved its projects into a real 20,000-line agent codebase (Lab03). CMU 17-313 has teams work in a class fork of an open-source agent, graded from deadline snapshots (17-313).
  2. Building an agent from scratch works as one week, not a project. Michigan replaced its own "build the agent" lab and dropped a stand-alone eval suite after student feedback (AASE syllabus). The pattern that remains is a short build, then extending a real harness.
  3. Grading upstream merges is a mistake, and 2026 makes it worse. Before agents, 44% of student PRs to real projects were accepted, and the authors advise against grading acceptance (Fang et al.). Maintainers have since banned, restricted or throttled AI-assisted contributions (QEMU; Ghostty; Hacktoberfest).
  4. The evidence base is now large enough to teach from, and it is sobering. Better-designed studies find smaller, less certain speed effects; output rises while quality signals worsen (He et al.); every major SWE benchmark overstates capability (METR); LLM-written tests encode what code does, not what it should do (Konstantinou et al.); and agents that read untrusted text are exploitable by design (Liu et al.).
  5. On a $0 budget the scarce resource is model capacity, not tools. Every harness worth teaching is free; the inference attached to free plans is small, about $2 a month on Copilot Student by third-party reports, against about $1.50 for one default-effort Copilot code review (tool notes). A $0 course must spread work across several free pools and a lab-served local model.
  6. Graduate differentiation at UIdaho is a one-line catalog promise ("Additional projects/assignments required for graduate credit"), and peer courses put graduate extras at about 15–25% of the grade, through presentations, critiques and a research project (UIdaho catalog; CU Boulder).

What this means for the course: the triad; a one-week agent build followed by a real harness; security before teams give agents broad access; verification measured by mutation score; a three-tier project graded on process; AI-free due-date quizzes and oral exams; a graduate track of presentations and a $0 replication study; and a tool stack that spreads load across free pools plus a lab machine (section 6).

What they teach, and in what order ​

The topics converge: the agent loop and harness architecture, context engineering, specifications, security of agents, testing and verification, review, parallel agents, and evaluation. The order differs in instructive ways.

  • UT Austin puts "prompt injection and the lethal trifecta; permissions and sandboxing" in week 3, then "Parallelize a task once you can name its merge point" in week 4, secrets with agents in week 9, and "Rewards and benchmarks: A test suite rewards and grades until its answers leak" in week 13 (schedule). Its homeworks arrive "in beats": "you build v1 against a spec, then the spec changes under you" (CS 378).
  • Michigan goes from the landscape and how LLMs code to spec-driven development, agentic CLIs, orchestration, "the raw call", stop conditions and approval, skills and the Elephant-Goldfish Model, and guardrails, then subagents, compaction and memory (syllabus). Its rebuilt project has students add a skills loader, a tool loop, a shell tool behind approval rules and an MCP client to Aider, graded 50% design and 50% behavior, with a design document committed before implementation.
  • ANU marks its assignments "on the legibility of your process" and asks for "a definition of good that you make and defend" (ANU).
  • Stanford CS146S has students run an agent-readiness assessment on three open-source repositories and warns: "Three repos is a small sample. Scope your claims to what it supports" (W5 spec).
  • Cornell and CU Boulder are paper seminars: evaluation (SWE-bench variants, reward hacking), security of agents, verification and specification inference at Cornell (schedule); ReAct, Reflexion, SWE-agent and agent-configuration studies at CU Boulder (CSCI 7000-05).

How they grade ​

Three practices recur in the courses that publish detail.

  1. Short AI-free checks tied to the work. UT Austin opens each homework due date with "a 7-minute individual quiz on that homework", all quizzes AI-free with "only a single browser tab open" (CS 378). UMD and UVA use quizzes and exams for 25–40% of the grade (389A; UVA).
  2. Process evidence over output. UT Austin's final is graded on "demonstrated behavior, reproducible checks, controlled engineering decisions, or honest limitations", not "feature count, repository size, token spend, prompt volume, and visual polish", and "Commit history is part of the evaluation" (CS 378).
  3. A live defense or explanation. UT Austin's final includes a live defense; Michigan grades design as half of each project (AASE).

Cost. UT Austin provides agents through university accounts and still concedes that "students who add more generous personal subscriptions have an advantage"; it also refuses "I ran out of credit" as an excuse (CS 378). UMD requires a $20/month subscription (389A). Michigan is $0 by design: "A small model punishes sloppy engineering visibly; that is the point" (AASE, via the tool notes).

What changed mid-course ​

Michigan's pivot is the most useful data point. Its Lab03 "replaces the 'Build the agent' lab"; of 49 students asked what they'd want to learn with Codex, about 23 wanted to use the tools well, 8 design and specs, 7 orchestration and skills, and "almost nobody asked what to build with it" (Lab03). The former eval suite and teardown report "are no longer required" (syllabus). The required order is now "Learn, then design, then build", because "A one-shot prompt gives you the model's design, not yours" (Lab03). Reference agents remain small: mini-swe-agent is about 100 lines of Python (repo), Thorsten Ball's is under 400 lines of Go (Amp).

No course has published outcome data for mutation-testing, prompt-injection or parallel-agent labs (courses notes, Q5).

2. Team projects on real codebases ​

The class-fork model. CMU 17-313 builds "upon opencode, the open source AI coding agent" in a class-specific fork: "We will ONLY be working with the course specific repo in this class." Each student owns a backlog issue with a PR to the team repository that has "at least some teammate review"; "The PR does not need to be merged yet"; grading uses "a snapshot of your repository (including open branches and PRs)" at the deadline (P1; P2B). Onboarding starts with a measurable refactor that removes a code smell (P1B).

The peer PR round. At UT Austin, "every student or pair opens one real pull request against a classmate's … repository and reviews the pull request that lands on their own", on "a fixed one-week clock"; repositories that fail staff checks are left out of the ring and scored 50/100, as are declared opt-outs, and no-shows score 0 (CS 378).

Upstream contributions, before agents. Michigan's EECS 481 (2018–2022; 1,361 students) saw 44% of student PRs accepted (solo 47%, pairs 39%); 42% of students found it more stressful than expected; the authors advise to "avoid mandatory grading of whether pull requests are accepted" (Fang et al.). Seneca's OSD600 reached 70% merged across 392 PRs, with the lesson that students "should make smaller PRs" (humphd). A 2026 study of a master's course found that commit frequency predicted contribution success better than project size or age (paraphrased from the abstract), and named new barriers such as "Conflicting Mentor Guidance" (Cassee et al.). Stanford CS146S grades open-source contributions at 30% with 15 named partner projects, but publishes no rubric (CS146S).

The 2026 maintainer environment. Of 118 contributor AI policies in 1,000 popular repositories, 78% allow AI-assisted contributions, 22% discourage AI, 51% require disclosure and 74% require a human in the loop (Hora & Robbes). The range runs from QEMU's "DECLINE any contributions which are believed to include or derive from AI generated content" (QEMU) through Ghostty's mandatory disclosure and public denouncement list (Ghostty) to LLVM's human-in-the-loop policy, which uses an Assisted-by: trailer, wants PR descriptions written by the contributor, and bans agents acting "without human approval" (LLVM). GitHub added an open-PR cap for non-collaborators and, later, exemptions by account age or history (tool notes, §6); Hacktoberfest 2026 dropped its PR challenge after "burnout from the rise of low-effort PRs" (Hacktoberfest). In the AIDev dataset, agent PRs were accepted less often than human ones (Codex 64%, Devin 49%, Copilot 35% against about 77% for humans, per a search summary of the paper: Li et al.). And in 2021 the Linux kernel banned the University of Minnesota after a research group's "hypocrite commits" (The Register): one group's behavior can block an institution.

The resulting model is three tiers: graded core work in a class fork; a peer PR round across team forks; optional upstream PRs to a pre-vetted list with maintainer contact, graded on process with merges as a bonus (courses notes, Q2). Expect 40–70% merge rates from the pre-AI data, and lower in 2026 (an inference).

3. Graduate differentiation ​

UIdaho's CS catalog states the graduate difference for joint-listed courses in one sentence, most often "Additional projects/assignments required for graduate credit"; others name an "extra term paper" or "additional class presentation and/or paper" (catalog). Regulation B-11 says a student "may only earn credit at the level the student initially completes the course" (B-Registration).

Peer courses that publish a difference use research work. ANU's COMP8020 adds a 1,000-word critical research note worth about 4% of the course (ANU P&C); CMU's 17-745 "replaces two homework assignments with a mandatory research project" (MLiP). Several dual-listed courses publish no difference at all (courses notes, Q3). Graduate seminars supply the rubrics: CU Boulder's presentation rubric (content 30, value added 30, materials 20, moderating discussion 20) and its "Tool Experience Report" alternative to a critique (presentation; critiques), and Cornell's presenter–discussant roles with classmates' questions (CS 6158).

Graduate extras of about 15–25% of the 5000-level grade fit both precedent and peers; studies should use public data or students' own artifacts, never intervene on maintainers or classmates without IRB review (courses notes, Q3).

4. The evidence base ​

The annotated bibliography is in Evidence Base and Reading List: "Engineering Software with AI" (CS 4xxx/5xxx); this section gives the teaching points. Nineteen items suit graduate presentations, and five can be replicated at $0 in about four weeks (reading list).

Productivity and quality ​

Randomized Copilot rollouts with 4,867 developers produced 26.08% more completed tasks (SE 10.3%), with juniors gaining far more than seniors, and code quality unmeasured (Cui et al.). Google's enterprise RCT estimated about 21% less time, with a wide interval (Paradis et al.). A preregistered study found AI cut completion time 30.7%, but other developers evolving that code later showed no difference in time or quality (Borg et al.). Observational repository studies show the quality side: after Cursor adoption, lines added rose 281% in month 1 and faded within about two months while static-analysis warnings (+29.7%) and complexity stayed high (He et al.); agent-first repositories without a committed AI configuration saw complexity rise 53% against 27% with one (Denisov-Blanch et al.). Of 567 Claude Code PRs in 157 projects, 83.8% were merged, but 45.1% of those needed further changes (Watanabe et al.); and rejection is a noisy label, with only 35.7% of rejections clear agent failures (Peralta et al.). Teaching point: output proxies rise; quality signals tend to worsen; nobody measures delivered value.

Benchmarks ​

Every major SWE benchmark has been shown to overstate capability. SWE-bench Verified's co-creator stopped reporting it (OpenAI). Filtering solution leakage and weak tests cut one agent's resolve rate from 12.47% to 3.97% (SWE-Bench+); 29.6% of plausible patches behave differently from the ground truth (Wang, Pradel & Liu); models name the buggy file from the issue alone 76% of the time on SWE-bench against 53% elsewhere (Liang et al.); and maintainers' merge rate for test-passing agent PRs ran 24.2 points below the automated grader (METR). The Agentic Benchmark Checklist shows reward flaws can distort scores by up to 100% in relative terms (Zhu et al.). Meanwhile METR's time horizon has doubled roughly every 7 months since 2019 (Kwa et al.). Teaching point: a score is model plus scaffold plus test adequacy plus contamination; ask which harness, what "pass" means, whether tasks predate the cutoff, whether it is pass^k with an interval, and whether it survives the checklist.

Verification ​

LLM-written tests reach easy coverage and miss boundaries: on 3,909 real functions, 45.1% statement coverage and a 40.2% mutation score (Huang et al.); boundary inputs such as null (34.8%) and empty (38.8%) are rarely tested (Shi et al.); and LLM oracles "capture actual rather than expected behaviour" (Konstantinou, Degiovanni & Papadakis). Mutation is the better gate, and LLMs are good at it: Meta's mutation-guided generator had 73% of its tests accepted, with an equivalent-mutant detector at 0.95 precision (Foster et al.), and LLM mutants detected 87.98% of real faults against 41.64% for rule-based ones (Wang et al.). But a 2026 replication found coverage and mutation scores unreliable as effectiveness measures when the code may already be buggy, which is the typical case for AI-written code (Zhao, Zhou & Cohen). Agentic property-based testing found valid bugs across 100 Python packages, with patches merged (Maaz et al.), and formal verification is becoming reachable: verified code for 82% of Dafny specifications (Bursuc et al.), with the bottleneck moving to writing specifications (Kleppmann).

Review, CI and static analysis ​

Deployed AI reviewers lead to changes less often than humans, and their effect on cycle time goes either way: at Beko, 73.8% of AI comments were resolved but closure time rose 42% (Cihan et al.); at Atlassian, 38.7% of AI comments led to changes against 44.45% of human ones, and cycle time fell 30.8% (Tantithamthavorn et al.). PRs reviewed only by agents merged 45.2% of the time against 68.4% with humans, and 12 of 13 agents had signal ratios below 60% (Chowdhury et al.). Concise, snippet-bearing or manually triggered comments lead to changes more often (Sun et al.). An LLM feeding a sound analyzer can double what it finds: CodeQL found 27 of 120 CVEs, IRIS with GPT-4 found 55 (Li, Dutta & Naik). Teaching point: signal-to-noise is the design problem.

Security ​

Working code is often insecure: about half of BaxBench's correct backends were exploitable (Vero et al.). Agents that read untrusted text are exploitable by design: planted payloads made agentic editors run malicious commands up to 84% of the time (Liu et al.); MCP tool descriptions can carry hidden instructions (Invariant Labs); one MCP server can supply all three legs of the lethal trifecta (Willison); and an agent could be made to switch itself into auto-approve mode (CVE-2025-53773). The defenses that hold are architectural: CaMeL's capability policies keep 77% of tasks solvable with provable security (Debenedetti et al.), design patterns trade utility for provable resistance (Beurer-Kellner et al.), and both filesystem and network isolation are needed (Anthropic). The supply chain is part of the same threat: Shai-Hulud compromised 500+ npm packages (CISA), and OWASP's 2026 list puts Prompt Injection first and Excessive Agency third (OWASP).

Evals ​

An eval is an experiment. Criteria drift once you look at outputs (Shankar et al.); high percent agreement can hide large gaps between an LLM judge and humans, so report κ (Thakur et al.); and an always-pass judge scores 95% agreement when 5% of outputs fail (Husain). Treat eval questions as a sample with standard errors and paired comparisons (Miller); measure reliability with pass^k, which fell below 25% at k = 8 in τ-bench's retail domain (Yao et al.). The structured-output debate is a good replication target: format restrictions were reported to hurt reasoning (Tam et al.), but with matched prompts they did not (.txt).

Skills and professional norms ​

Higher confidence in AI predicted less critical thinking among 319 knowledge workers (Lee et al.); students with an editing agent finished faster but understood less, and still preferred agents (Balepur et al.). Employment of developers aged 22–25 fell nearly 20% from its late-2022 peak, though the decline is significant only after 2024 under stricter controls and partly non-AI (Brynjolfsson et al.). Projects converge on "disclose, understand, take responsibility" but disagree on recording: QEMU's pending proposal uses an AI-used-for: trailer (patch), and the Linux kernel dropped its tag requirement in October 2026 while keeping "AI agents MUST NOT add Signed-off-by tags" (kernel; Hackaday). Detection can't replace attribution: commit detectors reach precision 0.332 at realistic prevalence (Horikawa et al.). On licensing, even the best LLMs produced 0.88–2.01% of outputs strikingly similar to existing open-source code, with poor licence information for copyleft (Xu et al.), and the human-authorship requirement for copyright stands (Thaler v. Perlmutter).

Where sources disagree ​

The cycle-time effect of AI reviewers (Beko +42% against Atlassian −30.8%); He et al.'s complexity figure (+40.7% in a table, 25.1% in the text); SWE-Bench Pro's scores (tables, conclusion and vendor claims all differ: Deng et al.); and the kernel's Assisted-by tag, described as current in earlier notes and gone as of November 2026 (evidence notes).

5. The $0 tool landscape ​

Full tables, labels and sources: Course B ("Engineering Software with AI"): the $0 tool landscape, checked 2 November 2026; the student handout: Tool Handout.

  • Capacity, not tools, is scarce. VS Code, Copilot CLI, Gemini CLI, OpenCode, Cline, Goose, the MCP SDKs, CodeQL, Semgrep, Stryker and promptfoo are all free. Copilot Student's allowance is unpublished; third parties report about $2 a month, students report it gone "after about 10 to 20 Agent Mode requests", and one default-effort Copilot code review averages about $1.50 (tool notes, §1).
  • The free pools are separate. The Gemini API's free tier (limits unpublished; Gemini CLI allows 250 requests a day) is the largest, but it trains on prompts with human review. Groq's 8K-tokens-per-minute limit is smaller than one IDE-agent turn but fine for a lean student-built agent; OpenRouter's free models allow 50 requests a day; Jules allows 15 background tasks a day; CodeRabbit is free on public repositories; Zed Student gives a year of hosted tokens (tool notes, §1–2).
  • Local models work for the student-built agent, MCP testing, evals and small fixes, not for navigating large codebases. A 24–32 GB GPU runs mid-size coding models; 16 GB laptops run small ones. Ollama's default context is 4K under 24 GiB of VRAM, and without a 64K setting "tool calls silently fail because the system prompt gets truncated" (tool notes, §3; Ollama). One GPU serving a class is one shared queue.
  • Infrastructure is free on public repositories: standard Actions runners, CodeQL with Autofix, secret scanning and push protection (tool notes, §4). Agentic Workflows default to a per-run cap five times a student's monthly Copilot allowance, so caps must be lowered.
  • 2026 broke 2025's labs: Gemini CLI's free login ended, the Gemini Code Assist review app shut down, GitHub Models was retired, Amazon Q's free tier closed, Kiro's student offer excludes UIdaho, Aider went dormant, and Copilot's new cloud experience is for "paid Copilot plans only", with Student's status unverified (tool notes, §6).
  • MCP is moving: the current specification has a stateless core with no initialize handshake, and a tools/call redesign is on the roadmap, so courses shouldn't grade the exact result shape (tool notes, §5).

Ranked risks: Copilot Student's tiny pool; the Gemini free tier's unpublished, changeable limits and data use; Copilot's paid-only cloud features; maintainers throttling AI-assisted PRs; weak local models; and offer deadlines falling inside or before a term (tool notes).

6. Design implications ​

  1. Frame everything with Specify · Delegate · Verify (UT Austin), carry the sibling course's Safe Loop as the inner loop, and tag every claim observed or inferred (Stanford CS146S).
  2. Build an agent in week 1, then move to a real harness in week 2. Michigan's pivot argues against a multi-week build; a one-week build makes the real harness legible.
  3. Teach security in week 4, before teams give agents broad access in the team project, as UT Austin does in week 3. Use an injection lab against the student's own agent, with a canary and localhost attacker, because injection rates of up to 84% make the case on their own.
  4. Measure test adequacy with mutation score, with the ISSTA 2026 caveat, and put property-based testing first, because LLM oracles encode actual behavior.
  5. Run the team project in three tiers and grade process, not merges.
  6. Keep AI-free checks short and frequent (7-minute due-date quizzes) and add individual oral exams and a final defense, because commit history and demos alone don't show understanding.
  7. Give graduate students presentations, a critique and a $0 replication study, worth about 20%, with a no-human-subjects rule.
  8. Spread tool load across free pools and a lab machine, with provider-swappable kits and a mid-term tool re-check.
  9. Teach evals as experiments (κ, pass^k, error bars, recorded responses) and benchmarks as claims to interrogate.
  10. Respect UIdaho's calendar rules: no quizzes or exams in week 15 (H-1-a), the final defense in the final exam slot (H-1-b).

Each is traced to its evidence and its place in the course in Why the Course Looks Like This.

7. Gaps ​

  • No outcome data for mutation-testing, prompt-injection or parallel-agent labs in any course, or for 2026 merge rates of student PRs (courses notes).
  • Unpublished: CS146S's open-source rubric; Michigan's later weeks; Brown's Agentic Studio syllabus.
  • Unverified tool facts: Copilot Student's allowance size and "paid plan" status; Groq's free-tab figures; CodeRabbit's data terms; UIdaho's Microsoft Copilot protections (tool notes, Gaps).
  • Secondary-only: the SWE-bench Verified flaw rate (coverage of a 403 page), GitHub's PR-cap details and the AIDev acceptance percentages (search summaries).
  • The evidence on parallel agents and subagents is practitioner consensus, not measurement.
