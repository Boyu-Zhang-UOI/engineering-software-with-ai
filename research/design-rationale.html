Why the Course Looks Like This ​

The main design decisions in Engineering Software with AI, the evidence behind each, and where each appears in the course. For instructors adapting it, for curriculum reviewers, and for students who want to know why they're asked to work this way. Evidence comes from the November 2026 landscape report and its three research notes. Where a decision rests on practitioner consensus or a single course's experience rather than a study, the table says so.

The short version ​

Teach engineering judgment around agents, not the agents. Upper-division courses now agree on a shape: build or dissect an agent early, then work inside a real codebase; teach security of agents before giving them broad access; verify with measured test adequacy; review at scale; grade evidence of process rather than output (report §1). The research agrees with that shape: output rises with agents while quality signals worsen, benchmarks overstate capability, LLM-written tests agree with the code's bugs, and agents that read untrusted text can be steered (report §4). And on a $0 budget the binding constraint is model capacity, so the course spreads work across free pools and a lab machine (report §5).

How strong is the evidence? ​

  • Randomized and preregistered studies exist for productivity (field experiments with 4,867 developers, Cui et al.; Borg et al.) but not for teaching practices in courses like this one.
  • Large observational studies of repositories and PRs (Cursor adoption, AIDev, agentic PRs) show associations, not causes.
  • Benchmark and security studies are mostly preprints, several from vendors; they point the same way.
  • Course designs (UT Austin, Michigan, CMU, Stanford) are published syllabi and assignments; none has published outcome data for the labs this course borrows (report §7). Where the course copies a course practice, the evidence is "a strong course does it", and the table says so.

What we deliberately did not do ​

  • A multi-week build-your-own-agent project. Michigan tried and replaced it (Lab03).
  • Required upstream merges, or required upstream PRs at all. The pre-AI merge rate was 44%, and 2026 maintainers are more cautious.
  • A required paid subscription. UMD requires one (389A); this course's $0 rule rules it out, and department credits are an optional variant.
  • AI detectors. Detection of agent commits reaches a precision of 0.332 at realistic prevalence (Horikawa et al.); the course asks students to explain instead.
  • Grading the exact MCP result shape. The specification's tools/call result is on the roadmap for redesign (tool notes).
  • Copilot as the main agent. Its student allowance can't carry agent work (Tool Handout: The GitHub Student Developer Pack).

Open questions ​

  • Does a week with a small agent students run, read and extend, plus a real harness, teach agent internals better than either alone? No course has measured it.
  • Do injection labs change what students do later with agents, or only what they know?
  • Is mutation score a useful grading target for AI-written tests, given the ISSTA 2026 result? Graduate studies can help answer this (replicable studies).
  • How will 2026's maintainer policies affect optional upstream PRs? Record merge and response data each term (process data only, no study of maintainers).

How to update the course ​

  • A tool changed: update Tool Handout only, and its date. If a lab can no longer run on any free tool, the lab changes.
  • New evidence: add it to a dated note in notes/, then to the reading list and, if it changes a decision, to this table.
  • A new term: re-run the instructor's pre-term checklist, including the class-codebase gates.
