The Team Project ​

A semester of real engineering in a real open-source codebase: in teams of 3–4, you specify a contribution, delegate parts of it to agents, verify it to a standard a maintainer would accept, and defend it at the end. You are graded on that process, never on whether a stranger merges your code.

Weeks2 to 16 (finals week). Teams form in week 2; the final presentations are in the final exam period
Teams3–4 students. CS [4xxx] and CS [5xxx] students may be on the same team, with the same team expectations
CodebaseThe class codebase: a class fork of a real, active open-source project, chosen by the instructor before the term (The Class Codebase)
Where you workYour team fork: your team's own fork of the class fork, in the course's GitHub organization
MilestonesM0 proposal (end of week 5) · M1 first PR (end of week 8) · M2 major contribution (end of week 12) · Final (finals week): Team Project Milestones

The three tiers ​

The design follows what comparable courses learned. CMU's 17-313 builds on a class fork of a real agent codebase and grades "a snapshot of your repository (including open branches and PRs)" (17-313). UT Austin's CS 378 runs a peer PR round in classmates' repositories (CS 378). And Michigan's EECS 481, after five years of student PRs to outside projects (44% accepted), advises courses to "avoid mandatory grading of whether pull requests are accepted" (Fang et al.). So:

TierWhat you doWhenGraded as
1. Core work in your team forkYour team picks a feature area and issues in the class codebase, specifies them, and builds them through PRs to the team fork, each reviewed by a teammate and checked by CI. Staff grade a snapshot of the fork at each deadlineWeeks 2–16M0, M1, M2 and Final
2. The PR roundEvery student opens one PR on another team's fork; the receiving team reviews it and decidesWeeks 9–10Individually, in Labs and assignments (The PR Round)
3. Optional upstream PRsA change already reviewed in your team fork, offered to the parent project (or a short pre-vetted list), only where that project's written policy allows disclosed AI assistanceWeeks 12–15A small bonus if merged, never a penalty (Contribution Rules)

Tier 1 is where almost all of the grade comes from, and it depends on nobody outside the course. Tier 3 is a real-world bonus with real-world rules: maintainers in 2026 are overloaded with AI-generated pull requests, and the course's reputation, and the University's, rides on each one.

Teams ​

  • Forming teams. In week 1 you fill in a short Canvas survey: your languages, your weekly availability, the kind of work you want (core logic, tests and CI, tooling, documentation), and up to one person you'd like to work with. The instructor forms teams of 3–4 and announces them in week 2, balancing experience and schedules.
  • The team charter. In week 2 your team writes course/TEAM_CHARTER.md (template): how you communicate, when you meet, how you split and review work, what "done" means, and what you do when someone falls behind or you disagree. Keep it current; M0 checks it.
  • Rotating roles. For each milestone, one member is the integrator (keeps main green, merges approved PRs, runs the milestone checklist) and one is the verifier (owns the CI gates and the mutation and eval numbers). Rotate them, so everyone does each at least once.
  • Graduate students on a team do the same team work as everyone else; their graduate work is separate (graduate/).

How the work flows ​

Every change goes through the same path, which is also how most open-source projects work:

  1. An issue in your team fork, with a "done when" and an owner, linked from course/TASKS.md.
  2. Specify · Delegate · Verify in course/AI_LOG.md before you start: what's specified, what an agent will do with which permissions, how you'll check it.
  3. A branch named [github-username]/[issue-number]-[short-name], worked in the Safe Loop: small steps, tests, reading every diff.
  4. A PR to the team fork's main, with a description you write yourself (🔴 AI off) and the AI disclosure section filled in (PR template). Commits made with AI help end with Assisted-by: [tool and model].
  5. CI runs the project's tests (and, from week 9, lint and CodeQL or Semgrep). A red build is not ready for review.
  6. A teammate's review (REVIEW template), with located comments; you answer every one with a change or a reason.
  7. Merge by the integrator, once the review approves and CI is green.

Where things live ​

Your team fork is a copy of someone else's project, so keep the course's documents out of the project's own files. That way any change can later go upstream (Tier 3) without dragging course paperwork along.

WhatWhere in the team fork
The project's own code, tests and docsWhere the project keeps them
AGENTS.md (instructions for agents working in this fork)The repository root, where agents look for it. If the project already has one, add a clearly marked course section instead of replacing it
The team's course documents (listed below)A course/ folder at the root. Those with a template in templates/ start from it (for example SPEC.md template and AI_LOG.md template); the others follow the outline in their week's lab
CI for the course's gatesThe fork's workflow files, kept separate from the project's own workflows where possible (.github/workflows/course-gates.yml, week 9)
Grades, peer evaluations, reflections, the final report (its draft in week 14, the report in finals week)Canvas only, never the fork

The files in course/, by the week that starts them:

  • course/TEAM_CHARTER.md: week 2.
  • course/THREAT_MODEL.md: week 4, updated in week 10.
  • course/SPEC.md, course/DESIGN.md and course/TASKS.md: M0 (week 5), kept current to the Final.
  • course/AI_LOG.md: from the team's first task on, a Specify · Delegate · Verify entry for each change (step 2 above).
  • course/SECURITY_REVIEW.md: week 10.
  • course/EVAL_PLAN.md: M2 (weeks 11–12), if your contribution uses an LLM.
  • course/RETROSPECTIVE.md, with its data and script in course/data/: week 13.

Your onboarding report (week 2) is individual, so it isn't in the team fork: it goes in course/ONBOARDING.md, with the agent's map in course/architecture-map.agent.md, in your own fork of the class fork (week 2).

Agents in the team fork ​

The agent safety contract applies in full. In the team fork it means:

  • Work in a Codespace or a dev container, with approvals on. The class codebase is someone else's code: open it with an agent only in a sandbox.
  • Agents never push to main. They work on your branch; you open the PR and you write its description.
  • Tokens are narrow: fine-grained, for your team fork only, short-lived. No agent gets a token that can reach another team's fork or the upstream project.
  • Background and cloud agents (week 13) run only on your team fork or your own fork, never against another team's fork or upstream, and every PR they draft is reviewed by you before anyone else sees it.
  • No unattended posting. No agent opens issues, comments or PRs in another team's fork or upstream on its own.
  • The data rule: the class codebase is public open-source code, so free tiers are fine here. Personal data and secrets never go into a prompt.

How it's graded ​

  • Milestones M0 (15 points), M1 (30), M2 (40) and Final (65) have team rows and individual rows (rubrics). The PR round (20) is individual.
  • Snapshots. At each deadline staff tag your fork's main (m0, m1, m2, and final at the start of the final exam period), record the open branches and PRs, and grade that. Open, reviewed PRs count; nothing has to be merged upstream.
  • Peer evaluations on Canvas with M1, M2 and Final, together with the contribution evidence in the fork, can adjust a member's share of the team rows (how).
  • The final presentation (finals week) is a ten-minute team presentation with a live demo (The Final Presentation). There is no final examination.
  • What isn't graded: lines of code, number of PRs, how much an agent did, tokens spent, and whether anyone upstream merged anything.

AI use on the team project ​

LabelOn the team project
🟢 AI onCode, tests, refactors, exploring the codebase, CI configuration, AI reviewers running alongside your own review
🟡 AI limitedSPEC.md (you write the criteria; an AI may critique them), DESIGN.md (an agent may explore and propose options; the decisions are yours), TASKS.md and AGENTS.md (you write them; an AI may critique them), THREAT_MODEL.md (an AI may brainstorm attacks; the analysis is yours), and the final report (AI may help with tables, figures and checking claims against the fork; the limitations, reflection and contribution sections are written by your team, 🔴)
🔴 AI offPR descriptions and review comments you sign; peer evaluations; the reflection parts of the final report

The complete list is in the AI use policy. Where the parent project's AI policy is stricter, it wins (contribution rules).

If things go wrong ​

  • The build or the test suite breaks after a sync: tell the instructor; staff fix the class fork and announce a known-good commit. Don't pull from upstream yourselves.
  • The codebase is harder than your plan assumed: shrink the scope at the next milestone and say why in course/TASKS.md. A smaller, verified contribution beats a larger, unverified one, and the rubric says so.
  • An agent did something you didn't approve (pushed, deleted, posted): stop, revoke its token, write down what happened in course/AI_LOG.md, and tell the instructor. Reporting it is never penalized.
  • A teammate has gone quiet: use your charter's process first (a direct message, then a team conversation). If that doesn't work within a week, tell the instructor. Don't wait for the peer evaluation.
  • You're out of free credits: switch to a fallback or the lab machine (troubleshooting). It isn't a reason for a late milestone.
