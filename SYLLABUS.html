Prerequisites ​

CS 3383 Software Engineering. The course builds on its requirements, design, testing and team practices and does not teach them again. Students should be comfortable with git, the command line, and Python or JavaScript. No experience with coding agents is needed, and the lower-division course Building Apps with AI is not a prerequisite.

Course Description ​

This course examines how software is engineered when coding agents write much of the code. Students learn how large language model (LLM) coding agents work, how to give them context through specifications, AGENTS.md files and the Model Context Protocol (MCP), and how to secure them. The course then covers spec-driven development, tests as oracles and measured test adequacy, changes to legacy code, code review at scale, supply-chain security, LLM-based features and their evaluation, parallel and background agents, and the published evidence on AI in software engineering. Each week pairs a Seminar on concepts, readings and debate with a hands-on Lab. Throughout the semester, teams work in a class fork of a real open-source codebase: they specify contributions, delegate parts of the work to agents, and verify the results to the standard a maintainer would expect. Grades rest on evidence of verification and on each student's ability to explain their own work without AI.

Required Textbooks and Materials ​

No textbook. Weekly readings are listed in the reading list. Students need a laptop with a modern browser and several free accounts; the required cost is $0 (Tool Handout). Before the first Seminar, students complete the week 0 setup, which takes about 90 minutes.

Learning Outcomes ​

At the end of this course, students will be able to:

  • Explain how LLM coding agents work, including the model, context, tools, the agent loop, permissions and sandboxes, and run, read and extend a minimal agent.
  • Apply context engineering through specifications, AGENTS.md files and MCP tools, and measure whether agents follow them.
  • Evaluate AI-written code with tests whose adequacy is measured by property-based and mutation testing and enforced in continuous integration, and review AI-authored pull requests, AI reviewers and static analysis against known defects.
  • Change large existing codebases safely through comprehension, characterization tests and refactoring, and contribute changes that reviewers accept.
  • Analyze and reduce the security risks of AI-assisted development, including prompt injection, supply-chain attacks, leaked secrets and excessive permissions, and act responsibly toward users, maintainers and licenses.
  • Build and evaluate LLM-based features with structured outputs and calibrated evals, accounting for cost and latency, and assess published evidence about AI in software engineering.

Evaluation Procedures ​

Assessment ​

Grades are based on evidence: commit history, the AI log (AI_LOG.md), tests and checks that anyone can reproduce, an honest account of limitations, and explanations given without AI.

The assessment guide is part of this syllabus. It contains the rubrics, the conversion of points to grades, and the rules on attendance, absences and make-up work.

Final Examination ​

There is no written final examination. The final defense takes place in the course's scheduled two-hour final exam slot in week 16. Each team gives a six-minute demonstration of its contribution, including a live check that anyone could reproduce, and answers a question as a team. Each member then answers individual questions about their own work without AI. The defense is worth 30 of the Final milestone's 65 points (10 for the team demonstration and 20 for the individual questions), and taking it is required for a grade of C or higher (final defense). The final report, the third peer evaluation and the CS [5xxx] final paper are due at 11:59 pm on the last day of finals week.

Performance Standards and Grading Policy ​

Letter grades are A (90–100%), B (80–89%), C (70–79%), D (60–69%) and F (below 60%), without plus or minus grades (catalog Regulation E). A grade of C or higher also requires taking both oral exams and the final defense (details).

Assignments ​

  • Labs and assignments: weeks 1–13 each have a Lab checkoff and a take-home assignment, due before the next week's Seminar (by default at 11:59 pm the night before). In weeks 9 and 10, each student also opens a pull request on another team's fork during the PR round.
  • Due-date quizzes: a seven-minute quiz, taken without AI, opens the Seminar in weeks 2–14 and covers the work just submitted (quizzes).
  • Team project: teams of three or four work in the class codebase all semester. The milestones are M0 (week 5), M1 (week 8), M2 (week 12) and the Final (finals week), with peer evaluations at M1, M2 and the Final (team project; milestones).
  • Oral exams: two individual 15-minute exams without AI, in weeks 8 and 14 (oral exams).
  • Reading responses: 150 to 250 words on each week's reading, written without AI.

Graded personal writing, including reading responses, reflections, peer evaluations, the final report and all graduate writing, is submitted on Canvas and never placed in a repository (privacy of your work). Students who fall behind should first complete each lab's "Minimum to keep up" section and tell their team early.

Late Work Policy ​

Each student has two grace tokens; each extends one individual deadline by 48 hours, with no reason required. Each team has one team token for M0, M1 or M2. Beyond that, late work loses 10% of its points per day for up to three days. Tokens do not apply to quizzes, oral exams, the PR round, the final defense or other live events, which have their own make-up rules, and running out of free tool credits does not justify a late submission. The full rules are under late work and grace tokens.

Undergraduate and Graduate Course Expectations ​

CS [4xxx] and CS [5xxx] students attend the same Seminars and Labs and complete the same core work: every lab and assignment, the due-date quizzes, both oral exams, the team project and the final defense. Teams may include students from both levels. Graduate students are expected to show greater depth by presenting and critiquing research and by running an empirical study of their own, as the catalog requires for joint-listed courses ("Additional projects/assignments required for graduate credit"). For this reason the shared components carry less weight in the CS [5xxx] grade, and the graduate work makes up the remaining 20%.

Undergraduate Students (CS [4xxx]) ​

Undergraduates complete the shared work, weighted as in the CS [4xxx] column of the assessment table. In weeks with a graduate paper presentation, they post one question about the paper on Canvas before the Seminar, written without AI; this counts toward Seminar participation. The expected workload is about 8.5 hours a week: two 75-minute meetings and about six hours outside class.

Graduate Students (CS [5xxx]) ​

Graduate students complete all of the shared work and about 1.5 additional hours a week of graduate work, worth 20% of their grade (graduate work):

  • a 20-minute presentation of a paper from the graduate seminar list in one Seminar between weeks 3 and 14, and one turn as discussant with three prepared questions (6%: 4.5% for the presentation and 1.5% for the discussant role);
  • a critique of one paper in about 1,000 words, or a tool experience report, due before the week 13 Seminar (3%);
  • a small empirical study at no cost, alone or in a pair, that replicates or extends published work: a protocol in week 6, interim results in week 11, a draft in week 14, a 10-minute talk in week 15 and a four- to six-page paper in ACM format in finals week (11%). Studies may not involve human subjects without IRB approval.

In addition to the common outcomes, graduate students will be able to:

  • Analyze and present research in AI for software engineering, judging what a paper shows, how strong its evidence is and what it means for practice.
  • Design and run a small empirical study and report it in a short paper.

AI Use ​

AI tools are a required part of this course. Students direct coding agents and are graded on whether they can specify, verify, secure and explain the result. Every task carries one of three labels: AI on, AI limited or AI off. Quizzes, oral exams, the individual questions at the final defense, reading responses, reflections, peer evaluations, signed pull request descriptions and review comments, and graduate critiques are always AI off. Students are responsible for understanding, validating and explaining all work they submit; work a student cannot explain does not count as their own. AI help is disclosed in AI_LOG.md, in an Assisted-by: [tool and model] line in commit messages, and in the AI section of each pull request. The complete rules are in the AI use policy, and the agent safety contract governs how agents may be used.

Academic Honesty ​

Academic honesty is important at the University of Idaho because it is the way you improve over time. Academic dishonesty, which includes cheating and plagiarism, is not tolerated at the University of Idaho.

Plagiarism means claiming another's work as your own. That can happen in several ways:

  • Copying another's work in whole or in part
  • Quoting another's work without attribution
  • Paraphrasing another's work without attribution
  • Purchasing another's work from "study aid" websites

In this course it also includes using AI where a task is marked AI off, and hiding AI help that the AI Use rules ask you to disclose. Suspected academic dishonesty is handled under the University's Student Code of Conduct and Resolution Process (FSH 2300), which includes reporting it to the Dean of Students. If you have any questions about how to cite a source or credit an AI tool, please ask me.

University of Idaho Classroom Learning Civility Clause ​

In any environment in which people gather to learn, it is essential that all members feel as free and safe as possible in their participation. To this end, it is expected that everyone in this course will be treated with mutual respect and civility, with an understanding that all of us (students, instructors, professors, guests, and teaching assistants) will be respectful and civil to one another in discussion, in action, in teaching, and in learning.

Should you feel our classroom interactions do not reflect an environment of civility and respect, you are encouraged to meet with your instructor during office hours to discuss your concern. Additional resources for expression of concern or requesting support include the Dean of Students office and staff, the UI Counseling and Mental Health Center, or the UI Office of Civil Rights and Investigations.

Firearms ​

The University of Idaho bans firearms from its property with only limited exceptions. One exception applies to persons who hold a valid Idaho enhanced concealed carry license, provided those firearms remain concealed at all times. If an enhanced concealed carry license holder's firearm is displayed, other than in necessary self-defense, it is a violation of University policy. Please contact local law enforcement (call 911) to report firearms on University property. University of Idaho leadership remains committed to maintaining a safe work, living and learning environment on campus. We will not tolerate any threatening use of firearms or any other weapons. While authorized license holders may have familiarity and be at ease carrying a loaded firearm, we ask that they be aware that many people are not familiar with handguns and are uncomfortable in their presence.
